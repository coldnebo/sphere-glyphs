# The written word as image

Every glyph here is one stroke, inked by rules that run from formal to free. Neither idea is
new. Calligraphers in the Islamic world made pictures out of words, and calligraphers in China
and Japan made the motion of the hand into the subject of the work. This is a small gallery of
both, with every picture free to share.

![One loop, five ways: the brush model's dial, set loosely after the five scripts of Chinese calligraphy, from seal to cursive.](fig:dial)

## Gold on indigo

![Folio from the Blue Qur'an, North Africa, 9th–10th century. Gold and silver on indigo-dyed parchment. The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 2004.88. Public domain (CC0).](commons:Folio from the "Blue Qur'an" MET DP167100.jpg)

Kufic is the oldest Arabic script used for calligraphy: low, horizontal and measured, with
letters stretched or compressed to keep each line the same length. In the *Blue Qur'an* the
gold letters sit on parchment dyed with indigo, so that a page of scripture reads first as a
pattern and only then as text.[^blue]

Kufic's geometry was later pushed to an extreme. In **square Kufic**, letters are laid out on
a grid, like pixels, and words become tiles. On a minaret of the Shah Mosque in Isfahan, the
names Muhammad and Ali lock together into one repeating square.[^squarekufic]

**Look further:** the [Blue Qur'an](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_Quran), and
[square Kufic on Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Square_Kufic).

## A sultan's signature

![Tughra of Sultan Süleyman the Magnificent, Istanbul, about 1555: his name and titles wound into one emblem, filled with blue-and-gold vines. The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 38.149.1. Public domain (CC0).](commons:Tughra Suleiman.jpg)

The **tughra** was the Ottoman sultan's monogram, placed at the head of every decree. It
packs his name, his father's name, his title and a blessing into one fixed figure: three tall
uprights, two great loops sweeping out to the left, and tails running off to the right.

It is less a signature than a glyph. Each sultan had his own, drawn by a court specialist, and
its outline was so recognisable that people who could not read it still knew it on sight.[^tughra]

**Look further:** [tughra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tughra), and a
[second tughra of Süleyman](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tughra_(Official_Signature)_of_Sultan_S%C3%BCleiman_the_Magnificent_(r._1520%E2%80%9366)_MET_DP234753.jpg) at the Met.

## A ship made of names

![Calligraphic Galleon, 'Abd al-Qadir Hisari, Anatolia, 1766–67. Ink and gold on paper. The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 2003.241. Public domain (CC0).](commons:Calligraphic Galleon MET DP234017.jpg)

The gold hull of this warship is made of words: the names of the Seven Sleepers of Ephesus
and their dog, Qitmir, who in the Qur'an sleep for centuries in a cave under God's protection.
Writing their names on a ship was believed to keep it from sinking. The sea beneath is written
too, in a script so small it was called *ghubar*, "dust".[^galleon]

Pictures made entirely of writing, now called **calligrams**, were made from India to Persia
and were especially loved in Ottoman Turkey: lions, horses, storks, peacocks, mosques and
ships. Some Sufi orders hung them in their lodges as objects of contemplation.[^galleon]

**Look further:** [zoomorphic Arabic calligraphy on Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Zoomorphic_Arabic_calligraphy),
and [calligram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calligram) for the same idea in the West, from
Hellenistic pattern poems to Apollinaire.

## The lion of God

![Calligram in the shape of a lion, Vesal Shirazi, Persia, 19th century. Ink and gold on paper. Public domain.](commons:Calligram in the shape of a lion.jpg)

Ali ibn Abi Talib, cousin and son-in-law of the Prophet, was called *Asadullah*, "the Lion of
God". So in Persian and Ottoman calligraphy his name and the prayers addressed to him were
often written as a lion. The text runs through the body and legs; the letters' natural curves
supply the mane and the tail.[^lion]

The constraint is the same one every glyph here faces: the shape must come out of the stroke,
not be traced around it.

**Look further:** a lion that is also a prayer: an
[album page with the *Nad ʿAli*](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Page_from_an_album_with_the_Nad_%E2%80%9BAli,_a_prayer_to_%E2%80%9BAli_ibn_Abi_Talib,_cut-out_in_the_shape_of_a_lion.jpg)
attributed to Mir ʿAli Haravi, early 16th century, and more in
[Ali in calligraphy](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Ali_in_calligraphy).

## A portrait without a face

![Hilye, Hafiz Osman, Istanbul, about 1680 (illumination about 1900). Sadberk Hanım Museum. Public domain.](commons:Hilye by Hafiz Osman from Sadberk Hanim Museum.jpg)

A **hilye** is a portrait made only of words: a traditional description of the Prophet
Muhammad's appearance and character, laid out in a fixed architecture of panels. The Ottoman
calligrapher Hafiz Osman settled its form in the late 17th century: a round central text, the
names of the first four caliphs at the corners, a Qur'anic verse in a band below.[^hilye]

Where a painter would show a face, the hilye leaves the image to the reader. The layout, not
the likeness, is what everyone recognises.

**Look further:** [hilya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilya), and the
[Hilyes category on Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Hilyes).

## Cut in stone

![Stele of Mount Yi, small seal script: a Song-dynasty recarving after Xu Xuan's copy of the Qin inscription of 219 BCE; modern rubbing. The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 1977.375.7. Public domain (CC0).](commons:泰嶧山模本碑-Inscriptions from the Stele of Mount Yi (Modern Rubbings) MET DP-12237-002.jpg)

Chinese calligraphy is usually told as five scripts: seal, clerical, regular, running and
cursive. The first three are formal; the last two are fast.[^fivescripts]

**Seal script** is the oldest. When the First Emperor unified China, he had his deeds carved
on mountain steles in a standardised "small seal" script, traditionally credited to his
minister Li Si. Every line has the same width; curves are even and symmetrical; nothing
betrays the speed of a hand. It is writing that already looks engraved.

For a thousand years Chinese
calligraphers learned either from **steles**, carved stone known through ink rubbings, or from
**model letters**, brush-written originals and their copies. In the Qing dynasty a whole
"stele school" turned back to the carved scripts to escape the polish of the brush.[^stele]

**Look further:** [seal script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seal_script),
[clerical script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clerical_script), and the
[Stele of Cao Quan](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Stele_of_Cao_Quan) (185 CE),
the classic of clerical script.

## The regular hand

![A page from a Song-dynasty rubbing of the Inscription on the Sweet Spring at Jiucheng Palace, Ouyang Xun, 632. Public domain.](commons:KaishuOuyangxun.jpg)

**Regular script** settled in the Tang dynasty, and Ouyang Xun's inscription for the
emperor's summer palace became its model. Every character stands upright inside an invisible
square, every stroke is distinct, and the balance is exact enough that students still copy it
as their first lesson.[^ouyang]

What survives is a rubbing of a stone cut from his brushwork: a brush shape seen through a
chisel. That double translation is why the strokes look so clean, almost engraved.

**Look further:** [regular script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_script), and
[Ouyang Xun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ouyang_Xun).

## Slender gold

![Auspicious Cranes, Emperor Huizong of Song, 1112. Ink and colour on silk. Liaoning Provincial Museum. Public domain. Scroll sideways for the painting and the poem.](scroll:Songhuizong5.jpg)

Emperor Huizong was a poor ruler and a great artist. His own script, **slender gold**
(*shoujin ti*), is regular script pared to wire: strokes drawn fast and thin, with deliberate
knots where the brush pauses, turns and presses. Beside his painting of cranes over the palace
gate, the poem reads almost like metalwork.[^huizong]

It is the formal end of the dial pushed as far as it will go, and still unmistakably made by a
moving brush.

**Look further:** [Emperor Huizong of Song](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_Huizong_of_Song),
and his slender gold on the coins of his reign, such as the
[Chongning tongbao](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Chong_Ning_Tongbao_1.JPG).

## The Orchid Pavilion

![Preface to the Orchid Pavilion Poems, Wang Xizhi, 353; Tang-dynasty copy attributed to Feng Chengsu. The Palace Museum, Beijing. Public domain.](commons:LantingXu.jpg)

In the spring of 353, Wang Xizhi and forty friends floated cups of wine down a stream and
wrote poems as they came to rest. Wang wrote the preface, a little drunk, in **running
script**, and is said never to have matched it again. The original is lost, possibly buried
with an emperor; the copies are among the most treasured objects in China.[^lanting]

The character 之 appears about twenty times, and no two are alike. Running script is regular
script set in motion: strokes join, the brush stays on the paper between them, and the
connections show the order and speed of the hand.

**Look further:** [Lantingji Xu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lantingji_Xu).

## A sail in a gust of wind

![Poem Written in a Boat on the Wu River, Mi Fu, about 1095. Handscroll, ink on paper. The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 1984.174. Public domain (CC0).](commons:北宋 米芾 草書吳江舟中詩 卷-Poem Written in a Boat on the Wu River MET DP118649 CRD.jpg)

Mi Fu wrote this poem about boatmen hauling his boat through mud, and he wrote it on board.
He held the brush with his arm suspended, working from the elbow rather than the wrist, so
that whole columns swing.[^mifu] His friend Su Shi compared his writing to a sailboat in a
gust of wind, or a warhorse charging into battle.[^mifu]

Characters grow and shrink, tilt and recover. Ink runs dry mid-stroke, and the dry streaks are
part of the record: you can see where the brush ran out and where it was reloaded.

**Look further:** the [Met's object page](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/39919),
and [Mi Fu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mi_Fu).

## The mad monk

![Autobiography, Huaisu, 777. Handscroll, ink on paper, 7.55 m long. National Palace Museum, Taipei. Public domain. Scroll sideways to unroll it.](scroll:Huaisu - Autobiography.jpg)

At the far end of the dial is **wild cursive**. The Buddhist monk Huaisu is said to have
practised on banana leaves when he had no paper. In his *Autobiography* he copied out the
poems admirers had written about him, which describe him writing drunk, filling whole walls at
speed. The characters
dissolve into one continuous line that loops down each column and on to the next.[^huaisu]

Here the reader reconstructs the text from the motion, the way a glyph on the sphere is read
from its path. Legibility becomes a matter of knowing where the brush must have gone.

**Look further:** [cursive script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cursive_script_(East_Asia)),
and [Huaisu's Autobiography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huaisu%27s_Autobiography).

## One character, one breath

![“Virtue” (toku), Hakuin Ekaku, mid-18th century. Hanging scroll, ink on paper. The Metropolitan Museum of Art. Public domain (CC0).](commons:大字「悳」-Virtue MET DP700748.jpg)

Japanese Zen monks took the expressive end of Chinese calligraphy and made it a practice.
Hakuin Ekaku, who revived the Rinzai school in the 18th century, wrote single characters at
enormous size, fast and heavy, with the ink splashing where the brush landed.[^hakuin]

The goal was not beauty but presence: a record of one unrepeatable movement. The purest form
of the idea is the **ensō**, a circle drawn in one stroke. It is a loop, like every glyph here,
and no two are the same.

**Look further:** [Hakuin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hakuin_Ekaku), [ensō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ens%C5%8D),
and [Zen calligraphy on Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Zen_calligraphy).

## Where to look

The glyphs' brush model runs from **formal**, close to seal script, to **expressive**, close to
cursive. These collections hold thousands of works across the same range, and their images
are free to use.

- **[The Met collection](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection)**: hundreds of thousands of
  open-access images in the public domain (CC0), including most of the works on this page.
- **[National Palace Museum digital archive](https://digitalarchive.npm.gov.tw)**, Taipei:
  the former imperial collection, with many images under open licences.
- **[Wikimedia Commons: Chinese calligraphy](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Chinese_calligraphy)**
  and **[Islamic calligraphy](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Islamic_calligraphy)**,
  where each file page states its licence.
- **[Chester Beatty](https://chesterbeatty.ie)**, Dublin, for Qur'ans, hilyes and Ottoman albums.
- The **Shanghai Museum's Chinese Calligraphy Gallery**, which hangs the scripts side by side,
  from bronze inscriptions to wild cursive.

Every picture here is a faithful reproduction of a two-dimensional work long out of copyright.
Wikimedia Commons, the Met and most US collections treat such reproductions as public domain.
Each plate links to its file page, which gives the full provenance and licence.
