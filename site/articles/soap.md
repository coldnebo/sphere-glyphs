# Films of soap

Dip a loop of wire in soapy water and lift it out. The film that clings to it pulls itself as
tight as it can: of every surface with that edge, it finds the one with the least area. That is
a hard mathematical problem, solved by a bubble in a fraction of a second. The glyphs' film style
asks the same question of each loop; these are the people who asked it first.

![A glyph's loop, B2, spanned by its soap film: the least-area surface, computed for this page.](fig:filmcover)

## The blind physicist

![Soap films inside wire cubes and frames. Engravings from *Popular Science Monthly* 36 (1889–90). Public domain.](commons:PSM V36 D640 Surface tension test of film 1.jpg)

Joseph Plateau, a Belgian physicist, lost his sight in the early 1840s, possibly from staring
at the sun in an experiment on vision. He went on working for decades, with his son and his
son-in-law as his eyes, and spent much of that time on soap films.[^plateau]

Dip a wire cube and the films never meet in a point. They meet in threes, along lines, at equal
angles of 120°; and the lines meet in fours, at the angle of a tetrahedron's centre. Plateau saw
these rules in the films. They were proved mathematically only in 1976, by Jean Taylor.[^taylor]

**Look further:** [Plateau's laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plateau%27s_laws), and
[Plateau's problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plateau%27s_problem).

## Lectures for children

![Soap films on wire frames, as shown in lectures of the period. *Popular Science Monthly* 36 (1889–90). Public domain.](commons:PSM V36 D642 Surface tension test of film supported on wire frames.jpg)

Over the New Year of 1889–90, the physicist C. V. Boys gave three lectures on soap bubbles at
the London Institution, to an audience of children. His book of them, *Soap-Bubbles and the
Forces Which Mould Them*, has stayed in print for more than a century.[^boys]

The question behind the wire frames, *which surface of least area fills a given edge?*, is
called **Plateau's problem**. Jesse Douglas and Tibor Radó proved around 1930 that every closed
curve has such a surface, and Douglas received one of the first two Fields Medals for it, in
1936.[^douglas] That is the film the glyphs' film style draws.

**Look further:** Boys's book is free at
[Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/33370).

## Colour from thinness

![A soap film's colours: bands of thin-film interference across a film stretched over a glass. Public domain.](commons:Film of soap interference 3.jpg)

![A soap bubble over a park in New York. CC0.](commons:Bubbles-7.jpg)

A soap film is only a few hundred billionths of a metre thick. Light reflects from its front
surface and from its back, and the two reflections interfere: some colours reinforce, others
cancel. Which ones depends on the thickness and on the angle you see it from, so the colours
swirl as the film drains and thins, until it turns black just before it bursts.[^interference]

The film style's iridescence imitates this, with colour that shifts with an imagined thickness
and with the viewing angle.

**Look further:** [thin-film interference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thin-film_interference).

## Roofs of soap

![Frei Otto's German Pavilion at Expo 67, Montreal, on a German postage stamp of 1997, quoting him: "We seek an architecture that arises through processes like those that make nature's constructions." Public domain.](commons:Deutscher Pavillon Expo 67.JPG)

The German architect Frei Otto designed tents and roofs by dipping wire models into soap
solution and photographing the films that formed. A soap film is a membrane under even tension,
which is what he wanted for a roof of cable net and fabric.[^otto]

His German Pavilion for Expo 67 in Montreal, and the vast roofs over the Olympic Park in Munich
in 1972, grew from such models. He received the Pritzker Prize in 2015; he died the day before
it was announced.[^pritzker]

**Look further:** [Frei Otto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frei_Otto), and the
[Munich Olympic Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympiastadion_(Munich)).

## Sculpture from saddles

![Three glyphs' films: B2, A1 and C4, each the least-area surface on its loop.](fig:filmrow)

A soap film is curved like a saddle at every point, bending up one way exactly as much as it
bends down the other. The sculptor Brent Collins carved such saddles in wood for years. In 1995
he began working with the computer scientist Carlo Séquin, who recognised in them Scherk's minimal
surface, and wrote a program to design new ones; their collaboration ran for fifteen years and
produced wood carvings, models and a four-ton snow sculpture.[^collins]

**Look further:** Séquin's pages on [Brent Collins](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~sequin/SCULPTS/collins.html)
and the [Scherk-Collins sculpture generator](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~sequin/SCULPTS/scherk.html).

## Bubbles and clouds

The Argentinian artist Tomás Saraceno builds habitats of iridescent membranes and clusters of
bubble-like modules, suspended in galleries and in the air: *Iridescent Planet*, *Entangled
Orbits*, and the long series *Cloud Cities*, which draws on soap bubbles and the foam-like
structure of the cosmos.[^saraceno]

**Look further:** [Studio Tomás Saraceno](https://studiotomassaraceno.org), and
[*Cloud Cities – Nebulous Thresholds*](https://studiotomassaraceno.org/nebulous-thresholds/).
